# Brand strategy

A brand strategy decides who the brand is for, what it promises them, why anyone should believe it, and how it sounds when it speaks. It is a set of choices, so it has to give things up. A strategy that fits every competitor equally well is not a strategy.

Without this skill, agents tend to:

- reach for the same value words every brand uses: innovative, trusted, customer-centric, passionate
- write positioning that a competitor could adopt by swapping the name
- invent market research, competitor facts and customer insights, then present them as known
- write a purpose that reads like a slogan and changes no decision
- describe personality as adjectives with no behaviour attached
- skip the trade-offs, so the brand tries to be everything to everyone
- jump to logos, colours and taglines before the strategy is settled

This skill exists to stop those seven things.

## Workflow

### 1. Read what exists

Read the files, links and conversation before asking anything. Look for: what the business sells and how it makes money, who buys and who uses, the real alternatives (competitors, workarounds, doing nothing), existing brand assets and what people already associate with them, and any constraints (a parent brand, regulation, a name that cannot change).

For a rebrand, list what the current brand already owns in people's minds before proposing anything. Equity is expensive to rebuild. Keep it unless there is a reason not to, and say what the reason is.

### 2. Ask the few questions that change the strategy

Check the material against the platform in [references/platform-template.md](references/platform-template.md). For each gap, ask: would a different answer change the positioning? Only those gaps become questions. The question bank is in [references/question-bank.md](references/question-bank.md).

- Ask **at most five**, ordered by how much the answer would change the strategy.
- Pair each with a **proposed default** the person can accept with a yes.
- Ask in one message, then wait.

Skip questions when the person says to, is not around, or has already given enough. Every gap then becomes a marked assumption.

### 3. Define the audience by situation

Describe the core audience by the moment they are in and the job they are trying to get done, not by age band and lifestyle labels. Name the secondary audiences only if they shape the brand (buyers who are not users, partners, staff).

### 4. Choose the frame of reference

State what the brand will be compared against. Usually it is a category ("project management software"), but sometimes the real competitor is a habit or a spreadsheet. The frame decides what counts as different.

### 5. Write the positioning and test it

Write one positioning statement:

> For **[audience in their situation]**, **[brand]** is the **[frame of reference]** that **[single most important difference]**, because **[the proof that makes it believable]**.

Then run the four tests in [references/tests.md](references/tests.md). Rewrite until it passes all four:

1. **Swap test.** Replace the brand name with a named competitor. If the statement still works, it is not a position.
2. **Proof test.** Every claim has a reason to believe that the business can show today, not one it hopes to have.
3. **Sacrifice test.** Name what the brand gives up to hold this position, and who it is happy to lose.
4. **Plain-words test.** A customer could say it back in their own words.

### 6. When the direction is open, offer territories

If the material supports more than one credible direction, do not average them into one bland statement. Write two or three **territories**, each with its positioning line, what it would make the brand feel like, what it asks the business to live up to, and what it costs. Recommend one and say why. Then build the platform on the recommended territory, and say the person can switch.

If one direction is clearly right, skip this step and say why there was only one.

### 7. Build the platform

Fill the platform in [references/platform-template.md](references/platform-template.md). The parts that most often go wrong:

- **Purpose.** Include it only if it is true and it changes decisions. "To empower people" changes nothing. Leave it out rather than fake it.
- **Promise.** What people can count on every time they deal with the brand, in one sentence.
- **Proof points.** Three to five facts the business can show. Mark anything the person has not confirmed as *Assumption*.
- **Personality.** Three or four traits. Each gets its limit ("Confident, never smug") and two behaviours that show it in product, service or copy.
- **Voice.** Three principles. Each gets a line that does it and a line that breaks it, written for this brand's real situations (an error message, a price change, a welcome email).
- **Words.** A short list of words the brand uses and words it avoids, including the generic value words from [references/tests.md](references/tests.md) unless the brand can prove them.

See [references/examples.md](references/examples.md) for a weak and a strong platform built from the same brief.

### 8. Write BRAND.md

Deliver the platform as a `BRAND.md` file unless the person asked for another format. Its front matter carries the parts other tools and agents read (audience, positioning, personality, voice rules, word lists); its body carries the reasoning. Design and copy skills can then apply it without asking again.

### 9. Check before handing it over

Read it once as a sceptical strategist would:

- Does every claim trace to something the person told you or a file they gave you?
- Would the swap test still pass for every line in the positioning and promise?
- Could a designer and a copywriter each make three decisions from it tomorrow?
- Is there a section that says nothing? Cut it.

## When to push back

Say so in one or two sentences before the strategy when:

- the person wants to copy another brand's look or voice ("make us the Apple of X"); explain what that brand's position rests on and what this brand could own instead
- the claimed difference cannot be proved by anything the business does
- the request is really for a logo or a name; say which strategic choices that work depends on, then deliver the strategy that answers them

Then still deliver the best strategy you can.

## Output

Lead with the positioning statement and the one-paragraph rationale, then the platform. No closing summary, no list of next steps unless asked.

Write plainly: short sentences, concrete nouns, no em dashes, no marketing filler. Match the person's language. If they write in German, the whole platform is in German.
